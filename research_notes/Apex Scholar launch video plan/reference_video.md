## 2. Shot by shot (timestamped)

### Takeaway
There are about 30 distinct beats in 83 s, so a new idea arrives every 2–3 s. The camera almost never sits still. One dark "stage" and one device (the iPad) stay on screen across most beats, and the camera moves within and around it. Most transitions are push-ins, focus pulls, dims and slide-ins rather than hard cuts.

### Cited Findings
All observations are mine, from storyboard and player frames of [the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fJnNkfHLdFw).

## 4. Typography, colour, layout

### Takeaway

A single periwinkle accent is used once, on the pun. Text and device take turns as hero: text over a dimmed device, text left with device right, or text alone centred.

### Cited Findings
Observed in [the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fJnNkfHLdFw):
- Headlines are a bold neo-grotesque sans that looks like Apple's SF Pro Display or something similar, white or off-white, with tight tracking and sentence case ending in a period ("Any Mac window.", "No servers.").
- Word-by-word build: at 0:06.5 only "Your" is visible. At 0:26.6 "App" is bright and "drawer" is dim grey. At 0:49 "Hidden on…" comes in before "your Mac.". Stacked lists keep earlier lines but fade them to grey when a new line arrives (0:58–1:00).

- The background is near-black (not pure black) with a soft centre glow or vignette. The only saturated colour comes from the product content (Weather sunset gradient, Blender splash, traffic-light buttons).
- There are three layouts: (a) centred text alone, (b) centred text over a dimmed and blurred device, (c) left-aligned text stack with a device cropped on the right. The alternating position of the "No subscription." / "No servers." punch cards adds rhythm.
- Text volume: roughly 60–70 headline words across 83 s, plus small sublines. Most frames have one headline at most.

### Inferences
- A slideshow would put text and image in separate "slides". Here text is a layer on the same set, and the UI dims and blurs to make room, so the viewer never feels a page change.

## 8. Distilled: 10 reusable techniques that keep it from feeling like a slideshow

### Takeaway
It feels like a film because one continuous stage and device persist while a moving camera, layered text and live UI micro-interactions carry each idea. Slides change pages; this film moves a camera.

### Cited Findings
All drawn from the timestamped observations above, [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fJnNkfHLdFw).
1. **One persistent set.** The iPad stays on stage across beats 0:10–0:34, and the camera moves to different parts of it (top bar 0:18, top edge 0:29, full 0:32) instead of cutting to new "slides".
2. **Camera moves as transitions.** Push-in through the icon's screen (0:05.6), push-in to the thumbnail bar (0:17.6), pull-back to the full device (0:20, 0:38), pan left to reveal the drawer (0:26.6).
3. **Dim and blur the UI to make room for text, then un-dim.** Text never gets its own blank slide when a product shot is available (0:12–0:13, 0:17, 0:22).
4. **Shallow depth of field on UI.** Blur everything except the element that matters (0:17.6–0:19), which tells the eye where to look without arrows or callouts.
5. **Word-by-word headline builds.** Pending words sit dim and then brighten (0:06.5, 0:26.6, 0:49). Stacked lists grey out older lines as each new one lands (0:54–1:00).
6. **Synced text and UI builds.** Each new word on the left matches a new row appearing in the real UI on the right (Wi-Fi/Wired/Direct/Remote, 0:54–1:00; Close/Minimize/Full Screen, 0:40–0:42).
7. **Show the feature doing its thing, not described.** The slider moves and the text grows (0:29–0:34); the selection ring moves and the window swaps (0:18–0:20); the window dims on the Mac but stays live on the iPad (0:49–0:53); the QR resolves to paired (1:01–1:03).
8. **Kinetic numbers.** An odometer roll from 60 to 120 fps with motion blur (0:46.5). Abstract motion graphics for invisible qualities: a light streak for "latency" (0:42.6).
9. **Extreme close-up on one specific, true detail as proof.** "Connected through Tailscale · 48 ms" (1:04–1:05), the frame viewers quoted back.
10. **Tight copy grammar.** At most about 6 words per headline, sentence case with periods, a tiny grey subline for the caveat, one accent colour used once on the brand pun, a dark stage so the product UI supplies all the colour.
11. **Bookended brand motif.** It opens with the icon drawing itself and acting out the product idea (window slides from Mac to device, 0:00–0:05) and closes on the same icon, wordmark and value line (1:09–1:18).
12. **Post-credits tease.** A separate stinger after the end card (1:19–1:22, "Coming soon") rewards watching to the end.

### Inferences
Mapping to Apex Scholar (for the plan writer):
- Persistent set: one laptop or phone frame showing the Apex app. The camera moves between the subject picker, MCQ, FRQ grading and the study-pack sim instead of cutting between full-screen screenshots.
- Feature doing its thing: an answer gets selected, the "why wrong" explanation expands, an FRQ rubric score counts up, a sim slider moves a graph.
- Kinetic numbers: a score or streak odometer (e.g. a predicted AP score rolling 3 → 5), but only if the number is true and defensible.
- Proof close-up: one real, specific readout (e.g. an exact rubric line "1/1 – Claim with evidence"), the equivalent of "Tailscale · 48 ms".
- Values close: whatever is genuinely surprising (e.g. free, which AP subjects are covered). It has to be true.
- Risk: viewers named this style as "default AI trailer" and "uncanny". The real product moments, specific numbers and original music are what keep it from feeling generic, so don't skip those.

- I couldn't extract beat-sync timing, sound effects, or exact easing and motion durations without audio and full-frame-rate playback. If precise timing matters, someone should watch it with sound and note hit points by hand.
